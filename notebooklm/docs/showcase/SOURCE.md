## The source

A community learning center had a small garden beside its reading room. The garden was cared for by volunteers, but watering was inconsistent. Some days several people watered the same plants. On other days everyone assumed someone else had already done it.

The group decided not to solve the problem by guessing. First, they observed the garden for one week. They wrote down which areas became dry fastest, which plants were already receiving enough water, and when volunteers usually visited the center.

Next, they listened to the people who actually used the space. Younger volunteers wanted a plan that was quick to understand. Older volunteers wanted clear responsibilities. The reading-room team wanted the watering routine to avoid interrupting classes.

The group tested a simple plan for two weeks. They divided the garden into three areas, gave each area a clear label, and used a shared checklist. A person who watered an area marked the date so the next volunteer could see what had already been done.

At the end of the test, the group reviewed the checklist and talked about what worked. They found that the clearest labels were easier to follow than long instructions. They also noticed that one area still dried out sooner than the others, so they adjusted the schedule for that area instead of changing the whole plan.

The lesson from the project was not that one watering schedule is always correct. The useful method was: **observe first, listen to the people involved, test a simple plan, record what happened, and adjust only what the evidence shows needs adjustment.**

Showcase outputs should use **only the source above**.

# Demo Source — The Neighborhood Seed Library

**License:** CC BY 4.0 — project-authored demonstration source for Gemini Notebook Prompt Lab.

**Purpose:** This is a deliberately self-contained fictional case study. Showcase outputs should use only the information below. Do not treat the numbers or procedures as claims about seed libraries in general.

---

A neighborhood community center created a small **Seed Library** beside its reading corner. The goal was to help neighbors share seeds, try simple home gardening, and record what they learned from one growing season to the next.

The Seed Library began with **120 labeled seed packets** donated by local residents. Each packet had four fields: plant name, month packed, contributor initials, and a short growing note. Visitors could take **up to two packets per visit**. Returning saved seeds later was encouraged but not required.

The center used three simple rules:

1. **Take only what you expect to plant.**
2. **Keep labels with the seeds.**
3. **Write down one useful observation after growing, even if the plant did not succeed.**

On the **first Saturday of each month**, two volunteers hosted a 45-minute help table. New gardeners could ask how to read labels, organize a planting plan, or record observations. Experienced gardeners were invited to share practical lessons from their own attempts, but volunteers reminded everyone to distinguish personal experience from information printed on the seed packet.

During the first month, the group found three problems. Some packets were missing dates, a few paper envelopes became damp, and several visitors took more packets than they could realistically plant. The volunteers responded by introducing a standard label card, storing packets inside sealed boxes, and placing a sign above the shelf that said: **“Take what you can grow; share what you learn.”**

After **three months**, 46 households had borrowed at least one packet. Twenty-one households returned a short growing note. The center did not rank people by success or failure. Instead, it added the notes to a binder so future visitors could compare experiences and decide what questions they wanted to investigate next.

The organizers described the Seed Library as a **shared learning experiment**, not a competition. Its value came from three things working together: clear labels, small repeatable rules, and people contributing observations back to the community.
